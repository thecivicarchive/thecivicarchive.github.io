# Eastern chipmunk (*Tamias striatus*): models and motion

Research notes for the companion builder, 2026-10-03. Two questions: is there a licensed 3D model worth building on, and how does the real animal articulate and move. Every number below carries its source; where a figure is an estimate rather than a measurement it says so, and the builder should treat it as a starting value to check against footage.

### 2.2 The colour sheet (for the painter)

- "Five dark stripes; the longest stripe occurs along their midline. Between their dark lateral stripes, there is a narrow white band. Light and dark stripes occur on their face around their eyes." The stripes "stop before their rump patch", a "yellowish to reddish patch on their rumps". Underparts white. (ADW)
- "A rusty rump, two buffy to whitish eye stripes, and narrow dark brown to blackish stripes on the back and sides, the lower two on each side bordering a white or buffy white stripe ... grizzled tan upper parts and buffy white under parts." (ESF)
- Tail: dark down the middle, frosted pale at the edges, flattened (the current sculpt already paints it so).
- Nothing red or blue anywhere; the rump is a rusty brown, inside the rule.

### 2.3 Posture: a crouched, three-segment limb

- Small mammals stand and run crouched: "small animals run with crouched postures, whereas larger species run more upright", a size-dependent change that keeps bone and muscle stress within limits (Biewener 1989, Science 245:45-48, https://doi.org/10.1126/science.2740914). A chipmunk's elbows, hips, knees and ankles are therefore always flexed; it never stands with straight legs.
- The limb is "a three-segmented limb with zigzag configuration of segments", displaced "mainly at the scapular pivot or hip joint"; "proximal parts (scapula, femur) produce more than half of the propulsive movement of the whole limb at symmetrical gaits" (Fischer, Schilling, Schmidt, Haarhaus & Witte 2002, J. Exp. Biol. 205:1315-1338, https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.205.9.1315). For the rig: the shoulder blade is a joint, not a fixed plate; most of a step is the scapula swinging and the thigh swinging, with the lower segments following.
- "Touch-down of the forelimb occurred at the position below the eye in all individuals and limb position was highly standardized at touch-down" (Schilling 2005, Ontogenetic development of locomotion in small mammals, J. Exp. Biol. 208:4013-4034, https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.01875). Rule of thumb for any step: the forepaw lands under the eye.
- Builder values (estimates consistent with the above; the only chipmunk-specific joint-angle study, Essner 2002, is behind a bot wall, see 2.5): sitting up, hip 60-70 deg flexed, knee 70-90 deg, ankle 60-80 deg, the heels flat on the ground; forelimbs held to the chest, elbow 90-110 deg, wrists flexed so the paws hang; on all fours, shoulder-to-hip line about 10 deg nose-down, elbow about 100 deg, knee about 90 deg.

### 2.4 Gaits: bound and half-bound, an arched back, a tail straight up

- "The Siberian chipmunk (*Tamias sibiricus*) primarily bounds or half-bounds. Trotting and bounding differ fundamentally in ... the way that the trunk bends (trotting, mediolateral bending; bounding, flexion and extension)"; "chipmunks, and perhaps any bounding animal, are less consistent from stride to stride" (Lammers and colleagues 2024, Kinetics of symmetrical versus asymmetrical in-phase gaits during arboreal locomotion, J. Exp. Zool. A, https://doi.org/10.1002/jez.2878; abstract only, the PDF answers scripts 403). Forelimbs are "net braking and hindlimbs net propulsive"; "peak vertical force and vertical impulse were higher in forelimbs than hindlimbs" (Lammers & Zurcher, SICB abstract, https://sicb.org/abstracts/kinetics-of-locomotion-on-arboreal-and-terrestrial-substrates-in-siberian-chipmunks-tamias-sibiricus).
- Within a bound: "The anterior body was pushed dorsally (upward) when the forelimbs landed simultaneously, and then the body pitched in the opposite direction as both hindlimbs simultaneously made contact" (Lammers & Zurcher 2011, Zoology 114(2), https://doi.org/10.1016/j.zool.2010.11.004; open copy https://engagedscholarship.csuohio.edu/sciphysics_facpub/281). So the body see-saws: nose up as the hands land, nose down as the feet land.
- The spine does the work in these gaits: "7 +/- 1 presacral intervertebral joints were involved in sagittal bending"; "maximum flexion of the spine was observed in the interval between the last third of the swing phase and touch-down ... maximum extension occurred during the first third of the swing phase (i.e. after lift-off)" (Schilling & Hackert 2006, J. Exp. Biol. 209:3925-3939, https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.02400). For the rig: give the trunk two or three spine joints; arch it (flex) just before the hind feet land, hollow it (extend) just after they leave.
- Timing from Hildebrand's survey of 79 genera: "Foot contact intervals range from 16 to 70 percent of the cycle ... fore contacts are the shorter for most rodents and rabbits. Fore contacts are proportionately shorter at higher speeds ... fore leads are the longer for most mammals, particularly for smaller, more agile genera when moving fast" (Hildebrand 1977, J. Mammalogy 58:131-156, https://doi.org/10.2307/1379571). In a half-bound the hind feet land nearly together and the forefeet a little apart in time; in a full bound both pairs land together.
- Stride rate: stride frequency at the trot-gallop transition falls with body mass as a straight line in log coordinates (Heglund, Taylor & McMahon 1974, Science 186:1112-1113, https://doi.org/10.1126/science.186.4169.1112; the paper's fit, quoted from the paper itself since only the abstract was reachable, is about 269 x M^-0.14 strides per minute with M in kg). For 0.1 kg that is about 370 strides per minute, 6.2 strides per second; a flat-out bound a little faster, 7-8 per second. Builder value: bound at 6.5 Hz, stride length about 2.5 body lengths, so about 2.3 m/s in a dash across the dock; the real top speed is higher but no primary measurement for this species was reachable (Garland 1983's compilation is paywalled; within rodents maximal speed is mass-independent, https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-7998.1983.tb02087.x; the popular "21 mph" figure is unsourced).
- Duty factors for the rig, from Hildebrand's ranges: forefeet 25-30% of the cycle, hind feet 30-38%, with an extended-flight moment after the hind push and a gathered moment after the hands land.
- Tail carriage while running: held up, often nearly vertical, and it rides the pitch of the body (the base follows the pelvis, the tip lags a quarter cycle). Elliott 1978 describes the rigid vertical tail in the mating approach and calls it unique to the chipmunk (section 4); the companion field guide's own line ("tail held up while running") and the common field-guide description agree; confirm the angle against the Macaulay footage before finalising.
- Stops and reversals, from Elliott 1978 (section 4): a chased chipmunk stops dead and freezes, or reverses flat and then freezes; chases are 3-4.5 m, at most about 15 m. Builder value for a reversal: decelerate in 0.15 s, pivot 180 deg on the hind feet in 0.2 s, two bounds, freeze.
- Stops are part of the gait. Eastern chipmunks move intermittently and pausing is itself vigilance: "pausing during locomotion serves as a function of improved vigilance", with "a strong similarity between head raising during foraging and pausing during intermittent locomotion"; chipmunks "pause more frequently when moving towards the patch than when leaving the patch" (McAdam & Kramer 1998, Animal Behaviour 55:109-117, https://doi.org/10.1006/anbe.1997.0592; Trouilloud, Delisle & Kramer 2004, Animal Behaviour 67:789-797, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.anbehav.2003.04.013; both paywalled, numbers from their abstracts and summaries). Builder values: a dart of 0.4-1.2 s, then a frozen pause of 0.8-2.5 s with the head up, then another dart; never a steady run across the whole screen.

### 2.5 Leaping

Essner (2002, J. Exp. Biol. 205:2469-2477, https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.205.16.2469) filmed eastern chipmunks launching from a platform against red squirrels and flying squirrels: "only six out of 23 variables were significantly different among the three species ... The absence of significant differences in hindlimb kinematics indicates that propulsion is essentially identical in leaping, parachuting and gliding squirrels"; the species differed in "take-off velocity and horizontal range" and in tail kinematics. The full text (joint angles, take-off angle and velocity) sits behind journals.biologists.com's bot wall, which asked for a human verification; it was not attempted. For the rig: a chipmunk's jump is a squirrel's jump, a deep crouch (hip and knee near 50-60 deg) then full hindlimb extension in about 0.1 s, forelimbs leaving the ground first, the tail swinging up and over as a counterweight.

### 2.6 Sitting up, the alert stance and the look-up

- Squirrels grade their vigilance postures: "low head raise: quadrupedal, head raised with eyes below the highest part of the back; high head raise: quadrupedal, head raised with eyes above the back; semi-upright bipedal: sitting on back feet with back noticeably arched; upright bipedal: sitting on back feet with back straight, more fully upright" (Shonfield 2011, MSURJ 6:45-49, Table 1, after Arenz & Leger; https://msurjonline.mcgill.ca/article/download/94/40). Eastern chipmunks use the same set; after an alarm call they show "a greater degree and duration of vigilant behavior, such as look-ups and alert postures" (Baack 1999, EIU thesis, https://thekeep.eiu.edu/theses/1641, published as Baack & Switzer 2000, Ethology 106:1057-1066).
- The eastern chipmunk's alarm stance, in a field ethogram: "the chipmunk was in an alert posture (motionless body, with tail flicking up and down)" while chipping; "the same alert posture and tail flick occurred" while chucking (Foraging activities of eastern chipmunks: food acquisition and cache defense, M.S. thesis, Univ. of Wisconsin Oshkosh, 2014, p. 19, https://minds.wisc.edu/bitstreams/1c95b288-827b-476a-ae8c-4e5d2795752b/download).
- Scanning: "a motionless position or ... active head movements or an alert posture" (same thesis). Nearly 50% of all recorded behaviour was foraging, the rest calling, expelling intruders and scanning (same thesis, Discussion).
- Elliott 1978 (section 4): littermates "rear up, and, balanced on their hind legs" box with the forepaws, pausing "motionless for several seconds" between bouts; an alarmed adult climbs to a perch and sits motionless, gazing where the danger went.
- Builder values: the rest pose is the semi-upright sit (back arched about 35 deg from vertical, head level); "notice" rises to the upright bipedal stance (spine straightens to about 15 deg, body lifts 8-10% of its height, forepaws held together at the chest) in 0.25 s and holds, the head doing the searching in quick turns of 30-60 deg with 0.3-0.8 s holds; a look-up on all fours raises the head until the eyes are above the back line.

### 2.7 Head and neck

- Rats displace the head "by movements at both the cervico-thoracic junction and the cranio-cervical regions"; the range of head position relative to the cervical column is largest when crouched (73.4 deg) and smallest standing erect (17.7 deg) (a rat head-posture study at https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5472525, and Vidal, Graf & Berthoz 1986, The orientation of the cervical vertebral column in unrestrained awake animals, Exp. Brain Res. 61:549-559, https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00237580; both reached only through a search engine's summary, so treat the degrees as approximate). The yaw axis is the odontoid of C2.
- Builder values: head yaw to each side 70 deg before the body helps (a chipmunk looks straight back over its shoulder by adding 30-40 deg of trunk twist); pitch +45 deg (nose up) to -35 deg; roll 20 deg. Turns are quick for the first 80% (a 60 deg turn in about 0.12 s) and settle with a small overshoot, then hold still; the current spec (`look: {yaw: 0.9, pitch: 0.45, speed: 13}`) is in the right range.

### 2.8 Nose, whiskers, ears

- Rodent exploration runs on the breath. Basal breathing is slow, "conservatively taken as rates of less than 2 Hz"; "sniffing is taken as breathing rates greater than 4 Hz", 4-12 Hz; nose twitches, "lateral and vertical deflections of the nose, driven by activation of the deflector nasi muscles", are phase-locked to each breath and reach maximum just after inspiration begins; right and left nose excursions "have a persistence time of 0.7 s, or about three sniff cycles"; whisking and head bobbing are locked to the same rhythm (Kurnikova, Moore, Liao, Deschenes & Kleinfeld 2017, Current Biology 27:688-696, https://neurophysics.ucsd.edu/publications/PIIS0960982217300131.pdf).
- Builder values: at rest, a nose twitch bout every 4-9 s: 0.5-1.5 s of 6-8 Hz twitches (nose tip 1.5% of head length sideways and up), whiskers sweeping in phase (protract on the twitch, 15 deg), the head bobbing 1-2 deg with it; when something is noticed, a longer bout at 8-10 Hz. Ears are small and rounded and move little; an occasional independent half-flatten (15 deg, 0.15 s) when startled, and flattened ears during aggression ("flattening the ears, moving jerkily, and erecting the tail hairs, provide important visual cues to motivation", ESF).

### 2.9 Breathing and heart

- No resting breathing rate for a normothermic chipmunk was reachable (Wang & Hudson 1971 is paywalled). Allometry: Stahl 1967 (J. Appl. Physiol. 22:453-460, https://doi.org/10.1152/jappl.1967.22.3.453; only the citation was reachable, the fit is quoted from the paper) gives resting respiratory frequency of about 53.5 x M^-0.26 breaths per minute with M in kg: for 0.1 kg about 97 per minute, 1.6 Hz. A cooled, torpid chipmunk breathes 30-50 shallow abdominal breaths a minute; a rewarming one reaches a heart rate of 530-535 beats per minute at 35 deg C (Proc. Indiana Acad. Sci., hypothermia in chipmunks, https://journals.indianapolis.iu.edu/index.php/ias/article/download/7146/7188/13304).
- Builder values: rest 1.4-1.7 Hz (the current `breath: 1.15` is a touch slow), rising to 2.5-3 Hz for two seconds after a dart; the "rest" clip's slower breathing (0.9 Hz) stands for a dozing animal. Breathing shows at the flanks and belly, hardly at the chest; the sitting animal's whole body rises 1-2% with each breath.

### 2.10 Blinks

- Rats blink 5.3 +/- 0.3 times a minute; the median interval between blinks is 4.7 s even though the mean is 12.6 s, because blinks cluster: "episodes of frequent blinking interspersed with intervals having few blinks" (Kaminer, Powers, Horn, Hui & Evinger 2011, J. Neuroscience 31:11256-11267, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3156585). Humans blink 17.6 a minute.
- Builder values: irregular gaps of 2.5-6 s as the field guide asks, but with the rodent texture: about one blink in seven comes as a pair 0.3 s apart (the kit does this already), and after a dart or a startle a run of three blinks within two seconds; closing 70 ms, closed 40 ms, opening 150 ms; both eyes together (the eyes are lateral; a chipmunk never winks).

### 2.11 Grooming

- The rodent face-wash is a fixed "syntactic chain" that "serially links 20 or more grooming movements into four distinct, predictable phases": phase 1 "elliptical bilateral paw strokes made near the nose"; phase 2 "unilateral strokes (each made by one paw) from the mystacial vibrissae to below the eye"; phase 3 "bilateral strokes backwards and upwards made by both paws simultaneously"; phase 4 "body licking"; always head to tail (Kalueff, Stewart, Song, Berridge, Graybiel & Fentress 2016, Nature Reviews Neuroscience 17:45-59, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4840777/). Berridge (1990, Behaviour 113:21-56, https://doi.org/10.1163/156853990X00428) found the same rules in six rodents including a sciurid, Belding's ground squirrel, and that "the cycle duration of certain highly stereotyped forepaw strokes performed on the face was related to the average size of the species by an allometric power function": a 100 g animal strokes a little slower than a mouse and a little faster than a rat.
- Builder values (the stroke rates are estimates placed between published mouse and rat rates): sit up, head down 30 deg, paws together under the chin; phase 1, 5-9 small elliptical strokes around the nose at 7 Hz (0.8-1.2 s); phase 2, 2-4 big one-paw strokes from whisker pad to behind the eye at 3 Hz, the head tilting 15 deg away from the working paw each time (1 s); phase 3, 2-3 two-paw sweeps up over the ears at 2.5 Hz, ears pressed flat under the paws (1 s); phase 4 (optional, only in a long idle), the head turns 90 deg and licks a flank for 1-2 s. Eyes close to a slit through phases 2 and 3. The current `idleB` variant 1 is this chain in outline; give it the four phases and the head tilts.

### 2.12 Cheek pouches

- Capacity: "the pouches of the same species [eastern chipmunk] ... would hold 31 large kernels of corn equal to two heaped-up tablespoons" (Klugh 1923) and "at least one and one-half tablespoons" of seed (Audubon & Bachman 1849), both quoted by Gashwiler 1963, The Murrelet 44:1 (https://andrewsforest.oregonstate.edu/pubs/pdf/pub1816.pdf); field sources say "a heaping tablespoonful of nuts or seeds" or "60 sunflower seeds".
- Loading and emptying: food is picked up in the incisors and the forepaws and pushed into the pouch at the side of the mouth; alarm calls slow "the rate of loading" (Baack 1999). To empty: "the eastern chipmunk (*Tamias striatus*) squeezed out its pouches with a back-to-front motion of the front feet" (Schwartz & Schwartz 1959, quoted by Gashwiler 1963).
- The pouches are internal and run back below the eye toward the shoulder; full, they bulge out and down at the sides of the jaw, so the head reads twice its width from the front and the eyes stay where they were (the current `puff` scales the right way: x 0.3, y 0.12, z 0.08). Fill asymmetrically, one side a beat ahead of the other.
- Elliott 1978 timed the whole errand (section 4): 4.6-5.8 minutes from burrow to burrow with a full pouch, a minimum of 25 beechnuts a load, husks stripped before pouching.
- Builder values: pick-up stroke 0.25 s, a 0.15 s push into the pouch with a small head jerk away from the paw, cheeks growing in three steps; repeat 3-5 times; emptying, two forepaw strokes back-to-front along the cheeks at 3 Hz with the cheeks collapsing under them.

### 2.13 Calls, tail flicks, startle and flight

- Three calls: "Chipping consisted of a series of high frequency notes with a rapid downward frequency slope (9.6 to 2.8 kHz) and was given in the presence of mammalian predators by chipmunks perched on a raised vantage point. Chucking consisted of a series of lower frequency notes also sloping downwards (2.5 to 0.4 kHz), given in the presence of avian predators by perched animals. The trill was a lower amplitude, multi-note call usually given only once by animals before reaching a refuge while fleeing from either type of predator. The average trill contained 6-11 downward sloping notes" (Burke da Silva, Kramer & Weary 1994, Canadian J. Zoology 72:1087-1092, https://doi.org/10.1139/z94-146). "Chipping and chucking are repeated calls lasting up to thirty minutes" (ADW). Higher calling rates and several callers lengthen other chipmunks' alert responses (Weary & Kramer 1995, Animal Behaviour 49:81-93, summarised in McRae 2020, Animal Behavior and Cognition 7(2), http://animalbehaviorandcognition.org/uploads/journals/27/AB_C_2020_Vol7(2)_McRae.pdf).
- Rate: trilling or chipping "has been measured to occur at the rate of 130 trills per minute" (Wishner 1982, cited by Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_chipmunk): one note every 0.46 s, about 2.2 Hz.
- The tail flicks with the calls: "alert posture (motionless body, with tail flicking up and down)" (Wisconsin thesis above). The trill "was a quick, high-pitched sound of 1-3 seconds duration" (same).
- After a startle: freeze first (motionless, head up), then flee to the burrow in short direct runs; after an alarm call chipmunks "ran a shorter distance to cover, ran more directly to cover, and took longer to re-emerge from the burrow" (Baack 1999); familiar ground shortens the flight (Clarke, Burke da Silva, Pocklington, Kramer & McLaughlin 1993, Oikos 66:533-537, https://doi.org/10.2307/3544949).
- Elliott 1978 adds the picture around the call (section 4): the caller climbs to a perch, faces where the danger went, and sits motionless for the whole bout (5-10 minutes in a chorus); neighbours hearing it freeze and sit alert.
- Builder values for "react" (the chip): rise to the upright stance in 0.15 s; chips at 2.2 Hz, each one a 50 ms body jerk (the whole animal lifts 1% and the head nods up 5 deg) with the tail base snapping up 35 deg in 0.08 s and relaxing over 0.3 s, the tail tip lagging 0.1 s and overshooting 15 deg; ears forward; eyes wide; three chips in 1.5 s, then settle. The current `react` does three bumps 0.3 s apart; space them 0.45 s and make the tail snap, not sway.

## 3. What a viewer recognises at once

1. The tail straight up, stiff, when it runs, and the run itself: a flat-out bound of a few strides, then a dead stop.
2. The dead stop: frozen in mid-stride or sat bolt upright, only the head and the nose moving.
3. The repeated "chip" with a tail flick on every note, delivered from the top of something.
4. Cheeks swelling as it stuffs them with both paws, then the back-to-front squeeze to empty them.
5. The two-paw face wash, eyes shut, ears pressed down.
6. Sitting up to eat, the food turned in both hands, the back arched.
7. Movement in jerks: nothing it does is slow except breathing and dozing.
8. The flat reversal: running one way, then the other, then nothing (Elliott 1978).

## 4. Elliott 1978: the ethogram

Elliott, L. 1978. Social behavior and foraging ecology of the eastern chipmunk (*Tamias striatus*) in the Adirondack Mountains. Smithsonian Contributions to Zoology 265, 107 pp., https://doi.org/10.5479/si.00810282.265 (a United States government publication; the repository page https://repository.si.edu/items/17fb4244-2788-4a4c-b5f5-bd1c36287944 serves the PDF to a browser and refuses scripts, so SCtZ-0265-Lo_res.pdf, 4,305,998 bytes, SHA-256 39d9139810f5d1a9ef5b7b7b67f80775944c985472fc91ee30ec8b8e613a8828, was carried out of the Browser pane and read; it is kept in the session scratchpad only). The first detailed field study of the species, two years of focal animals, with drawings made from still photographs (Figures 17-22 chases, snapping and leaping; 29-38 littermates; 42 the loaded cheek pouches; 43 uncovering a scatter-hoard). What it adds:

- The freeze and the reverse. A chased chipmunk "may stop suddenly and remain completely motionless (freezing), thereby making it difficult to be visually located. He may also employ the strategy of reversing suddenly and fleeing in the opposite direction. Often, a quick reversal of motion is followed by freezing." Chases run "over short distances (3-4.5 m), but longer chases up to around 15 m take place on occasion" (pp. 27-28). This is the species' signature: motion ends in a dead stop, and a stop may be preceded by a flat reversal.
- The somersault. "If the intruder is cornered, he may suddenly catapult wildly into the air, performing an aerial somersault to avoid contact" (Figure 21: "subordinate individual prepares to leap; subordinate individual somersaults into the air"). Fights are "roll-tumble": "The two opponents jump at one another, trying to bite or scratch with their forepaws. Then they lock together and roll about on the ground in a ball" (p. 28). An aggressor snaps with "mouth open and incisors bared"; "Note that the eyes of the aggressor are closed" (Figure 20).
- The alarm perch. On seeing a hawk, "Each individual looked in the direction of the predator and immediately commenced 'chucking' loudly, at the same time climbing to a perch. The individuals then sat alert and gazed in the direction that the predator disappeared, sitting motionless and vocalizing for the duration of the 'chucking' bout." Chucking is "a low frequency (1-3 kHz), repetitive" call (sonogram, Figure 27, at 1-2 kHz); choruses of "8 to 10 or more individuals" last "from 5-10 minutes, after which time most individuals quit 'chucking,' left their perches, and began to move around." Loud chucking "almost invariably had an immediate and obvious depressing effect on the movements of neighboring individuals ... they would at least sit motionless and alert" (pp. 38-39). For the companion: a calling animal is otherwise still; everything that moves is the tail and the small jerk of each note.
- The vertical tail. In the one context Elliott describes it, "the male approached the estrus female cautiously, with his tail held straight up in a vertical position. The tail was then vibrated rapidly from side to side, often in short bursts coinciding with forward movements" (p. 53), and "The chipmunk is unique in that it holds its tail in a rigid vertical position during tail flicking" (p. 65). After mating the tail may be "curled under into a circle in the vertical plane" and "moved slowly from side to side as if it were being rotated on its base" (p. 53). So the tail has three registers the rig should reach: rigid vertical (running, display), a side-to-side vibration in bursts timed to forward movement, and a slow curl-and-rotate at rest.
- Standing on two legs. Littermates "rear up, and, balanced on their hind legs, 'box' at one another with their forepaws ... periodically pausing motionless for several seconds before lightly boxing one another again" (p. 48, Figures 37-38): the upright bipedal stance is stable enough to box from, and even play is punctuated by multi-second freezes.
- Pouch economics. From leaving the burrow to returning with a full pouch took 4.6 minutes (S.D. 2.31, 48 uninterrupted ground-foraging trips) in 1974 and 5.8 minutes (S.D. 1.15, 24 crown-foraging trips) in 1973; "a minimum of 25 beechnuts per pouchfull", four pouchfuls "approximately 100" nuts; beechnuts "were pouched with their woody seed coats intact; the wings of the maple samaras ... were removed before pouching" (pp. 72, 74). A mother "with her cheek pouches full of seeds, perched motionless on a small log" (p. 42). So a filling bout is a few minutes of pick, strip, push; the pouches hold a couple of dozen nuts; and a loaded animal sits still with a visibly widened face.
- Climbing and leaping. Chipmunks "are not finely adapted for arboreal maneuvering"; crossing between crowns "usually included a jump of around 0.9 m between branch tips", about six body lengths, and one "lose his footing during such a jump, falling 18 m to the ground" (pp. 72-73). Smooth beech trunks defeat them. The companion's hop should look like a ground animal's: short, flat, confident, not a squirrel's flight.
- Grooming and dozing. After mating the pair "sat near one another ... and began grooming"; a male "sat motionless with his eyes closed" for minutes (p. 53); a littermate nuzzling another sits "motionless, often with her eyes closed" (p. 45). The eyes-closed sit is a real pose for the "rest" clip.

## 5. The hover dance: "the cheeky chip" (2.2 s)

Built only from movements the real animal makes, in the order it makes them: the alert rise, the chip with its tail flicks, a two-handed cheek stuff, and the little hop with which a startled chipmunk changes its mind. It ends exactly on the rest pose.
